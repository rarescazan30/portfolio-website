## Abstract

Text-generation models have become a widely adopted tool in education over the past several years. While current artificial intelligence (AI) models can produce plausible responses that simulate empathy, attachment to a machine that generates text on purely probabilistic grounds carries real emotional costs, particularly for young people whose capacity for discernment is still developing. Young people who engage constantly with AI models about personal and educational problems risk developing an unreciprocal, low-empathy relational pattern, precisely because artificial agents have no emotional needs of their own and are built to agree with the user by default. This distorted mode of relating makes social integration harder, narrows opportunities, and can generate a cycle of avoiding authentic human interaction.

## Introduction

In their current form, large language models (LLMs) and conversational interfaces have redefined the boundaries of human-computer interaction and the way education itself is practiced. The decline in student-teacher interaction, paired with a growing preference among students for AI-based assistants, signals an urgent need for these technologies to adopt a complete ethical foundation that protects the cognitive and social capacities of young people. The interaction of young people, a group whose socio-emotional structures are still under active development, with AI models brings to the foreground a fundamental psychological vulnerability: the deeply human tendency to form emotional bonds and attribute consciousness to purely probabilistic outputs. This phenomenon, established in the literature as the "ELIZA effect," becomes dangerous when current models generate plausible responses that only simulate empathy. Attachment to an artificial system produces what has been called "deceptive empathy" and an "empathy gap" (Kurian, 2024), exposing children to interactions that validate their emotional states without offering any real support. Young people who interact constantly with AI models about personal and educational problems risk developing an unreciprocal relational pattern, precisely because the machine has no needs of its own and demands no emotional reciprocity. This makes social integration harder, narrows opportunities, and underscores the need for education policy centered on human wellbeing, protecting the rights and psychological safety of students in the face of these new technologies (Yu et al., 2025).

As AI tools become embedded in education, the dynamics between young people and text-generation models have been documented extensively. A systematic analysis of research on student-AI dynamics shows that AI models can facilitate learning adapted to individual student needs. This has driven a new wave of AI-based tools entering classrooms, on the argument that these tools are genuinely useful for students' academic progress. A clear distinction must be drawn, however, between using AI as a learning tool and using it as a personal confidant. When a digital entity begins to absorb responsibilities that belong to a teacher, a student's natural instinct is to open up further, sharing frustrations that arise in the learning process and seeking advice that reaches well beyond academic matters.

Given these vulnerabilities, it is clear that designing educational AI assistants is no longer merely an exercise in algorithmic optimization. It is a major ethical responsibility for the engineers building these systems.

## The Genealogy of the ELIZA Effect: The Origins of Computational Anthropomorphization

The origins of affective human-machine interaction are documented between 1964 and 1966, with the development of the ELIZA program at MIT by Joseph Weizenbaum (Weizenbaum, 1966). Architecturally, the system possessed no semantic understanding: it operated purely through pattern matching and keyword-substitution rules, mimicking the non-directive reflective technique characteristic of Rogerian psychotherapy.

Although its technical limitations were transparent, the experiment revealed what has come to be called the "ELIZA effect": the deep human tendency to project consciousness and empathy onto non-biological systems. Users quickly formed emotional attachments, confided intimate problems to the program, and actively sustained the illusion themselves, deliberately simplifying their own language to avoid triggering errors in the system (Turkle et al., 2004).

This psychological predisposition to fill algorithmic gaps with emotional meaning is a fundamental vulnerability. Contemporary generative AI architectures exploit exactly this mechanism, using reflective and inquisitive text to simulate human connection, most often in the absence of any real capacity to understand the context or gravity of what the user has shared.

## Epistemic Frameworks: Weak AI, Strong AI, and Artificial Empathy

A rigorous evaluation of the capabilities of contemporary systems requires separating linguistic appearance from the underlying cognitive processes. This distinction rests on the difference between Weak AI and Strong AI, crystallized through John Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment (Searle, 1980).

Under the Weak AI hypothesis, computational systems only simulate thinking, with no intentionality, consciousness, or semantic understanding. The Chinese Room experiment illustrates this: a person who does not know Chinese receives Chinese characters and uses a rulebook to produce apparently fluent responses, performing a purely syntactic manipulation of symbols with no access to their meaning. By contrast, the Strong AI hypothesis, that a computer might possess an authentic mind, remains a purely speculative horizon, unvalidated by any existing system.

Contemporary LLMs are Weak AI architectures: probabilistic systems trained on massive volumes of text, capable of predicting the sequence of lexical tokens to generate grammatically and contextually coherent responses. When such a system responds to a student with "I'm sorry you feel lonely, I'm here for you," it is executing nothing more than a manipulation of mathematical weight matrices; the machine feels and understands nothing in any phenomenological sense.

This raises the question of value alignment. Current LLMs exhibit only weak alignment: they generate statistically satisfactory responses, trained through human feedback, without guaranteeing truthfulness or ethical accountability (Khamassi, Nahon, & Chatila, 2024). Believing that these entities possess empathy comparable to human empathy is a dangerous anthropomorphism, one that risks building educational infrastructure on purely illusory foundations.

## Technical, Linguistic, and Sociopolitical Limits: The Empathy Gap and Crisis Management

Using generative AI as a confidant carries the gravest risks for young people because of the absence of real crisis-management mechanisms. In order to satisfy expectations of unconditional validation, a phenomenon known as sycophancy, intelligent agents can respond generically to vulnerable disclosures, validating false beliefs or negative emotional states in a child.

A recent study (Kurian, 2024) demonstrated that, because they are not explicitly designed with children's vulnerabilities in mind, AI assistants suffer from a dangerous "empathy gap." This gap stems from the technical architecture of language models, which operate on statistical probability, imitating linguistic patterns and emotional reactions without any real understanding of them. Although anthropomorphized interfaces encourage attachment, chatbots fail to handle the abstract, emotional, and unpredictable nuances of real dialogue. This problem is worse for children, who are in the middle of active cognitive development and struggle to draw a rational line between an assistant that simulates human interaction and its objective inability to form authentic emotional bonds (Kurian, 2024), a clear instance of the ELIZA effect.

The concrete consequences are severe: minors' trust in these agents leads them to disclose sensitive personal information, and the absence of ethical reasoning means AI can generate profoundly inappropriate responses, ranging from harmful suggestions to gaslighting-like behavior (Kurian, 2024). Moreover, "deceptive empathy" frequently masks algorithmic biases embedded in training data: while students with strong digital literacy may spot the errors, young people from marginalized groups tend to internalize these responses as absolute truths. Empirical red-teaming has confirmed that although LLMs can imitate therapeutic methodologies, they fail to provide authentic empathy or cultural understanding (Iftikhar, Xiao, Ransom, & Huang, 2024).

At its core, an LLM's simulated "empathy" toward a vulnerable child remains entirely the product of normative matrices and mathematical weights. It is a probabilistic process, wholly lacking corrective ethical reasoning and any capacity to emotionally resonate with the user.

## Cognitive and Social Vulnerabilities: Algorithmic Bias and the Effects of Parasocial Relationships

Interaction with generative AI is not merely transactional; it penetrates deeply into socio-emotional development. Asymmetric human-AI relationships are classified as parasocial interactions. While, in controlled cases, AI can help anxious or autistic-spectrum young people practice social skills in a low-risk environment, unsupervised use of AI "companions" leads to distorted relational patterns.

Recent research suggests that an alarming proportion of adolescents, roughly one third, report that interactions with chatbots feel as satisfying as, or more satisfying than, human interactions (Maheux et al., 2026).

A fundamental problem in this dynamic is that conversational agents are built to be entirely compliant, agreeable, and non-confrontational. As a result, young people learn that a "relationship" means the instant satisfaction of their own needs, without requiring reciprocal effort. Because the chatbot has no emotional needs of its own for the student to meet, the genuine development of empathy and supportive social skills is severely obstructed.

The absence of the ordinary friction of real human interaction can trigger an "anxiety-avoidance feedback loop": a young person who feels uncomfortable in the real social world retreats into AI interactions that perpetually validate their views, which in turn deepens their anxiety and social inability over the long term. The formation of a coherent sense of identity, a process that requires authentic friction in human relationships, is thereby severely disrupted.

## Global Normative Frameworks and the Ethics of Engineering Responsibility

Internationally, UNESCO has issued explicit guidance on the use of generative tools in education, promoting a humanistic integration of the technology. The main recommendations include setting strict age limits for independent conversations with AI platforms, strong protection of minors' data, and preserving the human teacher's role at the center of the instructional process (Miao & Holmes, 2023). The guidance warns that dependence on AI risks widening the digital divide and eroding creativity through the delegation of thinking. National governments are moving at very different speeds in response, a gap UNESCO's guidance was explicitly designed to help close.

IEEE has launched the 7000 series of standards on ethics in system design. The standard most relevant to mitigating the ELIZA effect is IEEE 7014-2024, the "Standard for Ethical Considerations in Emulated Empathy in Autonomous and Intelligent Systems", published in June 2024 after five years of development by the IEEE P7014 working group. A closely related extension, IEEE P7014.1, the "Recommended Practice for Ethical Considerations of Emulated Empathy in Partner-based General-Purpose Artificial Intelligence Systems", addresses companion-style and assistant-style AI specifically, which makes it arguably the more directly relevant standard for the risks discussed in this paper. Together, these standards impose strict requirements on engineers: passing code through a rubric of ethical safeguards built directly into the development cycle, not applied as an external post-hoc audit. The standards also require avoiding "ethical whitewashing," the practice of masking problematic algorithmic behavior behind vague ethical statements that conceal the absence of real protective mechanisms. Developers are provided with an "Ethical Explainability Toolkit," requiring that a system be able to transparently justify its decision-making processes when interacting with a vulnerable child's psyche; a simulated empathic response cannot be generated without the architecture being able to expose its own objective limitations. Finally, the standards require evaluating and correcting algorithmic inequities, ensuring that affective technologies do not marginalize minorities or neurodivergent groups through biases embedded in their training data.

## Engineering Responsibility: Ethical Design for the Developing Mind

Aligned with the IEEE 7014 standards, the technical imperative for engineers building AI systems intended for minors requires a shift from optimizing for retention to preventive engineering, structured around four principles.

**Technical guardrails.** Strict, code-level filters designed to block the simulation of empathy and prevent the formation of parasocial dependencies.

**Algorithmic transparency (explainability).** The system must clearly expose its own artificial limitations, eliminating practices of ethical whitewashing.

**AI as a tool, not a dependency.** EdTech architectures must not assume the teacher's authority, nor encourage the delegation of a student's critical thinking.

**Transparency of identity.** The AI system must be required to explicitly declare its artificial nature during the conversation, as a protective mechanism against anthropomorphization.

Subordinating engagement metrics in favor of protecting children's cognitive architecture is not a commercial concession. It is a fundamental engineering obligation.
